IATI data quality in the Pacific

The International Aid Transparency Initiative publishes the world’s most comprehensive record of development spending. But using it to answer basic questions — how much aid did a Pacific country receive? who is funding what? — requires navigating five systematic quality problems. This page quantifies each one from the live data and explains what the Pacific Aid Signal does about it.

Problem 1: recipient-country misattribution

high impact

When a donor runs a global or regional programme, they tag every transaction to every country mentioned in the activity — even when the money is explicitly declared for one country. A $100M programme that names 40 countries reports $100M against each country unless the publisher declares percentage splits.

Across the 14 Pacific island countries, 176,633 of 251,578 transactions (70.2%) are for somewhere else. Without correction, this creates absurdities: the US State Department appears as Tonga’s largest funder at $91 billion, when Tonga’s actual declared share of those global programmes is 0.005%.

Excluded: tagged here, money elsewhere (176,633)
Retained: money genuinely for this country (74,945)
Signal correction: reads the declared recipient-country percentage on every transaction. When a transaction declares that 0.005% of the spend is for this country, only that share is counted. When no percentage is declared, the transaction is included at face value (the publisher did not say it was for elsewhere).

Problem 2: data staleness

high impact

IATI publishes historical records, not real-time data. Some funders update monthly; others have not published a new transaction in over a year. When a funder’s data goes stale, their spending disappears from any recent-period view — not because they stopped spending, but because they stopped publishing.

15 of 29 funders in the Pacific have data more than a year old. The largest is Australia Aid, the single biggest bilateral funder in 9 of 14 countries, with no transaction dated after 30 June 2025 — a gap of 455 days.

Signal correction: tracks per-funder data currency across all 14 countries (see data freshness heatmap). For Australia, supplements stale IATI data by reading DFAT’s business notifications directly — catching events like the Strongim Ekonomi managing-contractor notification for Solomon Islands the same afternoon it was published, while DFAT’s IATI data for Solomon Islands was already 15 months old.

Problem 3: cross-publisher double-counting

medium impact

The same aid activity can appear multiple times in IATI when both the funder and the implementing partner publish it. A $50M programme reported by both the donor government and the UN agency implementing it shows up as $100M in a naïve sum.

Across the Pacific, $388M (4.1%) of $9.36B in active spend is likely double-counted, in 88 identified duplicate clusters. This is not a rounding error — it is enough to change the ranking of funders in any country and to distort sector totals.

Signal correction: identifies duplicate activities by matching publisher identifiers, titles, and amounts across publishers. Deduplicates by keeping the publisher with the most recent data. The methodology and duplicate clusters are documented in the pipeline code.

Problem 4: negative transactions

context needed

IATI transactions can be negative — representing refunds, adjustments, de-commitments, or corrections. These are legitimate records, but a naïve sum that ignores them will overstate spending, and a system that excludes them will miss real reductions in aid.

3,054 negative transactions appear across the 14 Pacific countries. In Nauru, negative transactions in recent periods can make the 90-day total appear negative — not because aid was withdrawn, but because a prior period’s adjustment was booked in the current window.

Signal correction: includes negative transactions in totals (they represent real adjustments) but counts them separately. When a country’s 90-day total is negative, the country page explains why.

Problem 5: quiet funders

medium impact

A funder who disbursed in the last year but has no transaction in the last 90 days is “quiet” — they are active but invisible in any recent view. This can mean their data is slightly delayed (benign), or it can mean a programme has stalled or been redirected (significant). Without flagging the gap, the 90-day picture silently omits them.

What the Signal does about it

The Pacific Aid Signal applies five corrections to raw IATI data before publishing anything. These are deterministic rules with no model calls at runtime — every correction is reproducible and auditable.

Why this matters now

IATI is actively reimagining its approach to data quality. The 2026–2030 Strategic Plan (Area D) shifts from publisher compliance metrics to user-centric data quality — asking whether the data is useful to the people trying to use it, not just whether it was published on time. An EU-funded project is building the tools for this new approach through 2026.

The problems documented on this page are exactly what that initiative aims to address. They are not abstract — they are what happens when you try to answer a simple question like “how much aid did Fiji receive in the last 90 days?” using raw IATI data. The answer without corrections is wrong by a factor of three or more.

In July 2026, Publish What You Fund tested Claude Code on IATI data and found three failure modes: hallucinations, missed double-counting, and context blindness. The Pacific Aid Signal addresses all three by design: no model calls at runtime (no hallucinations possible), cross-publisher deduplication (double-counting caught), and recipient-country weighting (context preserved).

Four AI tools now work with IATI data, each at a different layer: DevExplorer reads narrative documents (RAG), ChecKI checks publisher text quality, AidInsights answers natural-language queries, and the Pacific Aid Signal monitors transactions with data quality correction. The quality problems described here affect all of them differently — but they hit hardest at the transaction level, where a single misattributed activity can shift a country’s total by billions.
