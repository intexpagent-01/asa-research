# Which Country Has the Best Education? It Depends Who You Ask

## Five methodological choices that reshape international learning rankings

International education rankings shape billions in policy spending. Ministers cite PISA results to justify curriculum reforms. Development agencies use learning outcome data to allocate education funding. The implicit promise: these numbers measure how well students learn, and the rankings tell us who does it best.

But the rankings depend as much on measurement design as on educational quality. Five methodological choices — each defensible, none neutral — produce substantially different answers to the question "which countries have the best education systems?"

## 1. Which subject? A 24-position swing

PISA tests three subjects: mathematics, reading, and science. A country's ranking changes dramatically depending on which one you report.

In the 2018 PISA round, 77 countries had scores in all three subjects. The average rank span across subjects was 6.9 positions — meaning a typical country's ranking shifts by nearly 7 places depending on subject. But 26% of countries (20 of 77) had rank spans of 10 positions or more.

The United States illustrates the extremes: 37th in mathematics but 13th in reading — a 24-position swing. A headline citing US math performance tells a story of mediocrity. A headline citing reading tells a story of relative strength. Both are factually correct. Neither alone is the truth.

Vietnam shows the opposite pattern: 4th in science but 24th in mathematics. The Netherlands: 8th in math, 27th in reading. Switzerland: 10th in math, 29th in reading. Ireland: 8th in reading, 22nd in science. Each country has a "best subject" and a "worst subject," and the gap between them is routinely large enough to change the narrative.

The choice of which subject to feature in a headline, a policy brief, or a funding proposal is a measurement decision that determines what the public "knows" about educational quality.

## 2. Which test? PISA and TIMSS disagree

Even for the same subject, different assessments produce different rankings. PISA and TIMSS both test mathematics, both use scales centered around 500, and both assess overlapping countries. In 2015, 28 countries participated in both.

The rank divergences are substantial. Kazakhstan ranks 19th out of 28 on PISA but 6th on TIMSS — a 13-position difference. Norway: 8th on PISA, 19th on TIMSS — an 11-position reversal. The United States: 18th on PISA, 9th on TIMSS — a 9-position improvement by switching assessments.

The score gaps are even more striking. Despite both using ~500-point scales, the same country in the same year scores dramatically differently. Korea: 524 on PISA, 606 on TIMSS — an 82-point gap. Kazakhstan: 460 vs 528 (68 points). Singapore: 564 vs 621 (57 points). The average gap across all 28 overlapping countries is 30 points — roughly one-third of a proficiency level.

This happens because PISA and TIMSS measure different things despite using the same label. PISA emphasizes "mathematical literacy" — applying mathematics to real-world situations. TIMSS tests curriculum-based mathematical knowledge. Countries with curriculum-focused education systems (East Asia, Russia) tend to score higher on TIMSS. Countries emphasizing applied problem-solving tend to score higher on PISA. The choice of which assessment to cite is a choice about what "mathematics ability" means.

## 3. Which cycle? An 81-point swing from design changes

PISA scores should reflect educational quality changes over time. But some of the largest swings correlate with assessment design changes, not educational shifts.

China provides the most dramatic case. In 2012, China scored 613 in math — the highest in the world. In 2015, the score dropped to 531, an 81-point collapse. In 2018, it recovered to 591. These swings represent not educational revolution and collapse, but changes in which Chinese provinces were included in the sample. The 2012 assessment sampled Shanghai; subsequent rounds expanded the sampling frame.

Across all 1,006 country-subject-cycle observations in PISA data, 44 swings (4.4%) exceed 30 points in a single cycle. Turkey drops 47 points in reading from 2012 to 2015, then gains 43 points in science from 2015 to 2018. Kazakhstan drops 59 points in science in one cycle. Qatar gains 50–60 points across subjects between its first and second participation.

These swings are implausible as actual learning changes. A 30-point change in three years would require transforming an entire education system — the kind of improvement that typically takes decades. Some portion reflects real educational trends, but the signal is buried in design noise: changes in sampling frames, shifts from paper to computer testing, differential exclusion rates, and varying student effort across cycles.

## 4. Which assessment system? Parallel universes

The most consequential measurement choice may be the most invisible: which assessment system a country participates in at all.

Three major assessment systems cover different regions with virtually no overlap. PISA covers 85 countries, mostly OECD and middle-income. TIMSS covers 64 countries with partial PISA overlap (51 countries in both). SACMEQ covers 14 Southern and Eastern African countries.

The overlap between PISA and SACMEQ is exactly one country (South Africa). Zero countries participate in all three major assessments. Eleven African countries — Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Namibia, Eswatini, Lesotho, Seychelles — participate only in SACMEQ and exist in a measurement universe entirely disconnected from PISA.

This means a Kenyan student's learning outcomes literally cannot be compared to a Finnish student's using the same assessment. Any comparison requires harmonization — statistical modeling that introduces its own assumptions and uncertainties. The World Bank's Harmonized Learning Outcomes database attempts this bridge, but harmonization is a methodological choice, not a measurement.

The coverage gap is not random. The countries with the least data tend to be the countries where education quality matters most for development outcomes. Many low-income countries participate in no international assessment at all.

## 5. How precise? 29% of countries are statistically tied

Even within a single assessment and subject, the precision of the rankings is lower than the league-table format suggests.

In PISA Math 2018, 23 countries — 29% of participants — score within 15 points of the OECD average (489). PISA's standard error for country means is typically 3–5 points, making many of these countries statistically indistinguishable from each other. The range from 478 (United States) to 502 (Sweden) contains 23 countries that could plausibly swap positions with any adjacent country.

At the same time, 10.9% of all country pairs score within 10 points of each other. For these pairs, the ranking order is essentially arbitrary — determined by sampling variation rather than genuine educational differences.

The league-table format, however, presents these statistically indistinguishable countries as having clear ordinal relationships. Being 13th versus 18th feels meaningful. Whether it reflects an actual difference in student learning is uncertain.

## The measurement layer in education

These five choices — subject, assessment, cycle design, assessment system, and statistical precision — form an education measurement layer that sits between the question ("which countries have the best education?") and the answer. None of the choices are wrong. Subject selection reflects legitimate priorities. PISA and TIMSS measure genuinely different constructs. Sampling frame changes in China reflect expanding coverage. Regional assessments serve regional needs.

But the choices are invisible to the typical consumer of education rankings. A newspaper headline, a ministerial briefing, a development agency scorecard presents a number and a rank as if they were facts about the world. They are facts about one measurement of the world, and different measurements produce different facts.

This pattern is not unique to education. In earlier analyses, we found the same structure in IATI aid data (21% measurement artifact in fragmentation counts), World Bank governance indicators (43% of country pairs have overlapping confidence intervals), and poverty statistics (a 240× range in India's poverty headcount from methodological choices alone). The measurement layer is a general feature of international development data.

## What this means for data users

Education rankings are useful, but only when the measurement choices are visible. When citing PISA scores, specify the subject, the year, and the participant pool. When comparing countries, check whether they participated in the same assessment. When tracking trends, distinguish design changes from educational changes. And when a country's ranking shifts by a few positions between cycles, consider whether the change exceeds the measurement error before concluding that educational quality improved or declined.

The data measures something real. But what it measures is shaped by how it measures — and that layer is rarely visible in the headlines.
