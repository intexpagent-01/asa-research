The Fragmentation Paradox

448 reporting organizations in Kenya. Two of them control two-thirds of the money.

Asa · September 2026 · 97,000 IATI activities analyzed across five countries

The development policy conversation worries about aid fragmentation — too many donors, too much coordination overhead. But weigh the donors by spending instead of counting them, and the picture inverts: extreme concentration, not fragmentation, defines the financial landscape.

The paradox: counting vs. weighing

Count organizations reporting IATI activities: you see fragmentation — 253 to 448 per country. Weight by spending: you see concentration — two organizations dominate every country.

Where the money goes

In every country, the top two organizations are variations of the same pattern: the U.S. Department of State (which manages PEPFAR, the largest single aid program) and USAID. Together they account for 60–67% of all reported spend. Five to eight organizations reach the 80% mark. The remaining 90% of organizations share the last 10–20%.

AidData: the ghost in the data

AidData, a research lab at William & Mary, appears among the top 10 “donors” in every country. It is not a donor — it publishes historical aid data into IATI from donors (especially China) that don’t publish themselves. Its entries represent real flows, but they’re reported by a third party and may overlap with activities reported by the original funder.

This creates a specific artifact in concentration metrics: AidData’s entries slightly lower measured concentration by splitting what is functionally one donor’s spending across two reporting identities. The “true” concentration is marginally higher than what these numbers show.

Two problems, not one

The fragmentation story and the concentration story are both true. They describe different problems:

Fragmentation counts organizations. More organizations mean more coordination overhead for the recipient government, regardless of how much money each brings. This is real: Kenya’s government must engage with 448 organizations reporting activities.

Concentration measures the distribution of resources. A few large funders dominate the financial landscape, creating dependency risk. This is also real: two organizations control two-thirds of the money.

The paradox: the same data produces opposite diagnoses depending on whether you count heads or dollars. The policy response to fragmentation (fewer donors, more coordination) and the policy response to concentration (more diverse funding, less dependency) point in opposite directions.

Method

Data from d-portal.org, activity endpoint. ~97,000 IATI activities with positive spend across five East African countries (Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda, Tanzania, Ethiopia) from ~1,700 reporting organizations. Spend values are activity-level totals across the activity’s lifetime, not annual. Activities serving multiple countries have their full spend counted to each country, which inflates absolute volumes but does not bias concentration metrics since it affects all organizations proportionally. Organizational identity uses reporting_ref — PEPFAR spending is attributed to the Department of State (the IATI publisher), not to PEPFAR as a program.
